# Tomato Cultivation and Disease Management Guide

Compiled for AgriMind AI demonstration from standard extension recommendations. Adapt to local advice from your KVK or horticulture department.

## Climate and season
Tomato grows best at day temperatures of 21–28 °C. Fruit set is poor when night temperatures fall below about 13 °C or day temperatures stay above about 32 °C. In Karnataka tomato can be grown in Kharif (June–July sowing), Rabi (October–November) and summer (January–February) where irrigation is available.

## Soil and field preparation
Tomato prefers well-drained sandy loam to clay loam soil rich in organic matter with a pH of 6.0–7.0. Plough the field 3–4 times and incorporate 20–25 tonnes per hectare of well-decomposed farmyard manure. Raised beds help drainage in the rainy season.

## Nursery and transplanting
Raise seedlings on raised nursery beds or in protrays with sterilised cocopeat. Treat seed with Trichoderma or a recommended fungicide to prevent damping off. Seedlings are ready for transplanting in 25–30 days, at the 4–5 leaf stage. Common spacing is 60 × 45 cm for determinate varieties and 90 × 60 cm or wider for staked indeterminate hybrids.

## Nutrient management
Apply fertilisers according to a soil test. A typical recommendation for hybrids is in the range of 200–250 kg N, 100–150 kg P2O5 and 100–150 kg K2O per hectare, applied as a basal dose plus split top-dressings or through fertigation. Calcium deficiency causes blossom-end rot (dark, sunken patch at the fruit's bottom); maintain uniform soil moisture and adequate calcium.

## Irrigation
Irrigate immediately after transplanting, then every 5–7 days in winter and every 3–4 days in summer for furrow irrigation. Drip irrigation saves water and reduces foliar diseases because the leaves stay dry. Flowering and fruit development are the most moisture-sensitive stages; irregular watering causes fruit cracking.

## Major diseases

### Early blight (Alternaria solani)
Symptoms: dark brown spots with concentric rings ("target board" pattern) on older lower leaves, often with a yellow halo; leaves dry and drop. Also affects stems and fruit near the calyx.
Management: use healthy seedlings; remove and destroy infected lower leaves; crop rotation of 2–3 years away from tomato, potato and other solanaceous crops; mulch to stop soil splash; spray a recommended protectant fungicide such as mancozeb or chlorothalonil, or a systemic one such as azoxystrobin, according to the label.

### Late blight (Phytophthora infestans)
Symptoms: large, irregular, water-soaked, greasy grey-green patches on leaves that turn brown-black; white downy growth on the underside of leaves in humid weather; firm brown patches on fruit. Spreads very fast in cool (15–22 °C), humid, cloudy weather.
Management: avoid overhead irrigation; provide good spacing and staking for air flow; remove and destroy infected plants; spray protectively before favourable weather with mancozeb, and use systemic fungicides such as metalaxyl + mancozeb or cymoxanil + mancozeb as per label when disease appears. Do not plant near infected potato fields.

### Septoria leaf spot (Septoria lycopersici)
Symptoms: many small circular spots (2–3 mm) with dark brown margins and grey or tan centres with tiny black dots (pycnidia), starting on lower leaves.
Management: remove infected leaves; avoid working in wet fields; rotation; mulching; protectant fungicide sprays.

### Leaf mould (Passalora fulva, syn. Fulvia fulva)
Symptoms: pale green to yellow spots on the upper leaf surface with olive-green to brown velvety mould on the underside. Mostly a problem in polyhouses with high humidity (above 85%).
Management: improve ventilation, reduce humidity, avoid wetting leaves, use resistant varieties, and apply recommended fungicides.

### Target spot (Corynespora cassiicola)
Symptoms: small water-soaked spots that enlarge into brown lesions with light concentric rings, on leaves, stems and fruit; can resemble early blight.
Management: remove crop residue, improve air circulation, avoid excess nitrogen, and apply recommended fungicides.

### Bacterial spot (Xanthomonas spp.)
Symptoms: small, dark, greasy-looking spots on leaves, sometimes with a yellow halo; raised scabby spots on fruit. Spreads by rain splash and contaminated seed.
Management: use disease-free seed and seedlings; avoid overhead irrigation; rotate crops; copper-based bactericides (e.g. copper oxychloride) can slow spread. Fungicides do not control bacteria.

### Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV)
Symptoms: upward curling and yellowing of leaf margins, small crinkled leaves, stunted bushy plants and heavy flower drop. Transmitted by the whitefly Bemisia tabaci.
Management: there is no cure for infected plants — uproot and destroy them early. Raise seedlings under 40–50 mesh insect-proof net; use tolerant hybrids; yellow sticky traps; border crops such as maize; manage whiteflies with neem-based products or recommended insecticides rotated by mode of action.

### Tomato mosaic virus (ToMV)
Symptoms: light and dark green mottling (mosaic) on leaves, leaf distortion, reduced fruit set and uneven ripening. Spreads mechanically through hands, tools and infected seed; very stable in plant debris.
Management: use certified seed; wash hands and disinfect tools; remove infected plants; avoid tobacco use while handling plants; rotation.

### Two-spotted spider mite (Tetranychus urticae)
Symptoms: fine yellow stippling on leaves, bronzing, and fine webbing on the leaf underside in hot dry weather.
Management: spray water to raise humidity, conserve predatory mites, use wettable sulphur or recommended acaricides according to label.

## Major insect pest
Fruit borer (Helicoverpa armigera): caterpillars bore into fruits. Use pheromone traps (5 per acre), plant African marigold as a trap crop (one row for every 16 rows of tomato), release Trichogramma egg parasitoids, spray HaNPV or Bacillus thuringiensis, and use recommended insecticides only when the economic threshold is crossed.

## Harvest
Harvest at the breaker or pink stage for distant markets and at the red ripe stage for local markets. Yields of 25–40 t/ha are common for open-field varieties and higher for well-managed hybrids.
